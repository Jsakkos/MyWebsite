I commute by bike and Caltrain, and I started tracking the trains because I wanted to see how mine actually run. Most of the time the news is good: <Fig k="on_time_pct" /> of arrivals reach their station no more than 4 minutes behind schedule, which is what I count as on time. But the late ones aren't spread evenly. They gather in particular trains, on particular afternoons, on particular days of the week. Here are four patterns I didn't expect when I started, each with the chart that shows it and a link to check your own train.

The expresses are the trains everyone wants: fewer stops, a quicker ride. Northbound, they're also the least reliable trains on the line.

The least reliable of all is train <Fig k="worst_train" />, the <Fig k="worst_train_departs" /> <Fig k="worst_train_direction" /> <Fig k="worst_train_service" />. On <Fig k="worst_train_late_pct" /> of its weekday runs it's more than 4 minutes late at <Fig k="worst_train_last_stop" />, the last stop I see it reach. It has company: <Fig k="nb_in_worst_25" /> of the 25 least reliable weekday trains run northbound.

Taken as a group, across every stop and every hour of a weekday, northbound expresses are late <Fig k="express_nb_late_pct" /> of the time. Southbound expresses are late <Fig k="express_sb_late_pct" /> of the time, and locals <Fig k="local_nb_late_pct" /> northbound and <Fig k="local_sb_late_pct" /> southbound.

<LeagueTable />

Part of the reason is mechanical. An express usually can't overtake the local running ahead of it, so when that local is late, the express tends to be late too. When the train ahead is running 10 to 20 minutes late, the next train is late <Fig k="headway_close_late_pct" /> of the time if it's scheduled within 10 minutes behind, and <Fig k="headway_far_late_pct" /> of the time if it's scheduled 21 to 30 minutes behind. Expresses are usually the trains scheduled closest behind another one.

That's a pattern, not proof. Whatever slows the whole line also makes neighboring trains late together. But the way it depends on the gap between the two trains is hard to explain any other way.

<HeadwayGrid />

It also matters where I'm measuring. For a train heading into San Francisco, the last stop I can see is 22nd Street, and the run up to it is where northbound expresses lose the most time. So this ranking is partly a ranking of that final approach, which is also the part of the trip a commuter into the city feels most.

Most delay is small and scattered. The worst of it isn't. The ten worst days in the record hold <Fig k="worst_days_10_share_pct" /> of all the arrivals more than 15 minutes late.

The damage lasts well past the stop itself. Trains keep running late for hours afterwards; typically it takes <Fig k="stops_hours_to_normal_median" /> for the line to get back to normal. The longest stop I've detected held trains for <Fig k="stops_longest_hold_minutes" />.

The feed tells me where trains are, never why they stopped, so the cause of most of these is inferred from the shape of the data. My best inference is that most are strikes, when a train hits a person or a vehicle and the line has to stop. Three of them match public reports, so those I can name as confirmed: on October 24, 2025, Caltrain posted major delays for all trains; on February 3, 2026, a person was struck and killed by a train near Palo Alto; and on September 2, 2026, a train struck a vehicle near Burlingame. The rest are most likely similar, but that's an inference, not a fact. It's worth keeping in mind the next time a train sits still for an hour: sometimes the reason is far worse than a late commute.

<Trust>

Every number on this page comes from the tracker's nightly build, so they move a little from day to day. Here's what's behind them, and where they stop being reliable.

**How precise.** The 511 feed reports each train's position once a minute, and some positions are stale, so each arrival time is good to about a minute. That's why "late" means more than 4 minutes behind schedule: comfortably above the noise.

**Checked against 511's own record.** In October 2026 I compared my arrival times with 511's own independent record of when trains reached each stop. They agreed closely: a correlation of r = 0.96, with 97% of arrivals within two minutes of 511's. Those two figures are from that analysis; unlike everything else here, they aren't recomputed nightly.

**What's left out.** Each train's first stop, because a train waiting at its origin platform looks on time whatever happens next. Calls scheduled after midnight, because 511 drops every train from the feed at midnight. And days when the feed went stale, with too few positions changing from one minute to the next to trust; those days drop out of every figure. A line-wide stop on one of those days is invisible to me, and so is one that only holds trains at the terminals.

**"The end of the line."** The feed loses each train before it reaches its final station, so I never see the last arrival. When I say "the end of the line" or "the last stop I see it reach", I mean the last stop I can see, usually the one before the terminal.

**Causes.** Apart from the three public reports named in story three, the cause of every line-wide stop is inferred from the shape of the data, not known.

**Why this isn't Caltrain's number.** Caltrain counts its on-time figure at each train's final stop. I can't see that stop, so I count every stop along the way instead. That's closer to what a rider waiting on a platform feels, but it's a different measure, so my figures and Caltrain's aren't directly comparable.

The code and the analysis are [on GitHub](https://github.com/Jsakkos/caltrain-tracker).
